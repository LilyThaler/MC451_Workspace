## Reflection

I took media ethics over the summer, so I recognize a lot of the main ideas about how to correctly follow research ethics. Though I am used to considering examples used in media-related fields rather than human research, there are many similarities and connections I am able to make between the two. Similar to research, journalists also need to find ethical ways to obtain information and in cases where an interviewee doesn't want to be disclosed, they must do their best to keep the identity and information of the person confidential. And in filmmaking, there are many regulations in place for ensuring that writers, actors, and other workers are kept safe and provided for on set. Moral theories in general can be very objective and lead to different outcomes depending on how they are interpreted. Whether it's Bentham and Mill's utilitarian theory of the greatest happiness principle or Aristotle's virtue ethics, there are also universal morals that every moral theory deems immoral, specifically that of lying/deceiving and murder. Many people would likely agree, and I personally would consider knowingly withholding life-saving medication from syphilis patients or visiting people's homes under false pretenses as unethical. I'm intrigued by how the Belmont report converted these well-known moral theories into specific research-based legislation. Respect for persons is a requirement of Immanuel Kant's moral theory, and Rawls emphasized the importance of a just and fair society. Considering ethical practices in my own project about independent film success, I should look at public film review sites like Letterbox'd or widely available box office information rather than analyzing anything with percieved private messages.
